What the metrics mean

Housing burden lower is better

Median household's housing cost as a share of disposable income (gross income minus ~30% for Finnish tax + social security). Housing cost = mortgage payments + upkeep + property tax for owners, or rent for renters. 20% means the typical household spends a fifth of take-home pay on housing.

% overburdened (>40%) lower is better

Share of households spending more than 40% of disposable income on housing. 40% is the standard Eurostat threshold for "housing cost overburden" — above it, a household is considered financially stretched by housing. This is a tail measure: it catches the people in trouble, not the average.

Homeownership higher usually better*

Share of households that own the home they live in (vs renting). *Not always good: the Vienna and Swiss models deliberately have low ownership with excellent affordability, so read this alongside burden, not on its own.

% housed higher is better

Share of households with any home at all (owned or rented). The complement is effective homelessness in the model — households with no residence. Should stay near 100% unless supply collapses.

Price / income lower is better

Median home price divided by median gross annual income. The standard international affordability yardstick. ~3–4 is considered affordable, 7+ severely unaffordable (Helsinki reality is roughly 7–8). Watch the direction it moves between scenarios more than the absolute level.

Multi-owners lower is better*

Share of households owning 2+ properties (landlords / investors). *Higher concentration generally means more renters dependent on landlords and more wealth inequality, though some rental supply is necessary — context matters.

Median mortgage debt lower is better

The median household's total outstanding mortgage balance. High and persistent debt across scenarios signals the "long-loan trap" — people owing large sums for decades rather than paying down.

Wealth Gini lower is better

Inequality of housing wealth across all households, 0 to 1. 0 = everyone holds identical property wealth; 1 = one household owns everything. Computed as the Gini coefficient of each household's total property value. Finnish wealth Gini is roughly 0.6 in reality; treat moves of 0.02+ between scenarios as meaningful.

All metrics are the value in the final simulated year. The dashed grey line on each chart is the baseline; the coloured line is the current run. "▲/▼ vs baseline" on each card is coloured green when the change is in the better direction for that metric, red otherwise.
